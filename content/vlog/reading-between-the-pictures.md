*What the Photographs on a Gwoon's Walls Can Tell Us About a Teacher, and What They Cannot.*

There is an old expression that encourages us to read between the lines, to look beyond what is immediately presented and consider what might not be so obvious. I believe we should learn to do something similar when we walk into a Gwoon and look at the photographs hanging on its walls. We should learn to read between the pictures.

Anyone who has spent time in traditional martial arts schools knows what I am referring to. Photographs of masters, grandmasters, ancestors of the system, and instructors standing beside respected figures in the martial arts community. In many cases, these photographs represent genuine relationships, important memories, and a history worth preserving. I have photographs of people who have contributed to my own development, and I value them for precisely those reasons. But I have also come to recognize that what a photograph shows us and what we assume it represents can be two very different things.

We live in a time when connecting with people around the world has become easier than ever. The internet has allowed us to discover teachers, attend seminars, access historical information, and establish relationships that previous generations might never have had the opportunity to experience. There is tremendous value in that. But like a blade, which can be used to prepare a wonderful meal or cause harm, the value of an instrument depends largely on how it is used. The internet has made it easier to pursue knowledge, but it has also made it easier to pursue the appearance of possessing it. And in a world where photographs can travel farther than the relationships behind them, I believe this distinction deserves our attention.

Consider what happens when someone walks into a Gwoon for the first time. Before they have exchanged hands with the instructor, before they have experienced a lesson, and sometimes before they have even had a meaningful conversation, they may already be forming an opinion based on the photographs displayed around the room. They recognize a famous name, see the instructor standing beside a respected grandmaster, and naturally begin making assumptions. Perhaps they imagine years of personal instruction, a close relationship, or knowledge passed directly from one generation to another.

Sometimes those assumptions are justified. Other times, the relationship represented in that photograph may have consisted of a seminar, a brief visit, or a few encounters that could be counted on the fingers of one hand. The photograph itself may be completely authentic, but the impression it creates, or that someone deliberately encourages it to create, may tell a very different story.

That is why I believe we need to read between the pictures. Not to question every photograph or dismiss the importance of the people appearing in them, but to understand that a photograph is evidence of a moment, not necessarily evidence of a relationship, much less of knowledge or skill.

There is a considerable difference between meeting a master, receiving instruction from that master, and spending years developing under their guidance. I have met people who, through a relatively brief conversation or training session, have helped me understand something I had previously struggled with. Sometimes a few words from someone with considerable experience can change the way we approach our practice. Those encounters can be valuable, and I would never suggest otherwise. But I also would not confuse them with years of personal instruction.

Anyone who has seriously studied Ving Tsun understands that learning involves much more than being shown a movement or receiving an explanation. There is the practice, the correction, the repetition, and the frustration of realizing that something we believed we understood was not quite what we thought it was. A teacher may correct something today that takes us months to begin understanding. We return, practice, make mistakes, and receive further correction. Eventually, something starts making sense, only for us to discover another layer we had not previously considered. This process requires time, attention, and a willingness to be corrected. While years of training do not automatically guarantee understanding, standing beside someone who possesses knowledge certainly does not mean that knowledge has become our own.

This brings me to something I find particularly troubling. There are people who seem more interested in collecting photographs with respected masters than in investing the time required to actually learn from them. They travel, attend events, take pictures, and accumulate names that can later be displayed as evidence of their supposed qualifications. Again, there is nothing wrong with attending seminars or meeting respected teachers. The concern begins when these encounters are presented in ways that suggest a depth of instruction or a relationship that never existed.

I cannot help but wonder what motivates that behavior. If the skill is genuinely there, why should we feel the need to borrow someone else's reputation to validate it? And if the skill is not there, wouldn't our time be better spent developing it? There is nothing embarrassing about admitting that we still have much to learn. After decades of practicing and teaching Ving Tsun, I continue to recognize areas of my own understanding that require attention. I don't see that as a weakness. I see it as part of taking the art seriously. What I find difficult to understand is the desire to appear to have accomplished something rather than making the effort to actually accomplish it.

But my greatest concern is not necessarily with the teacher seeking recognition. It is with the student who may be persuaded by it.

When someone walks into a martial arts school hoping to learn, they generally lack the experience necessary to evaluate the instructor's understanding. That is precisely why they are seeking instruction. Naturally, they look for signs of credibility. Titles, certificates, organizational affiliations, and photographs with famous masters can all influence their judgment. Unfortunately, sometimes the student is so impressed by the people on the wall that they forget to examine the person standing in front of them.

I think that is worth considering, because the student will not be spending the next several years learning from the grandmasters in those photographs. They will be learning from the person who has agreed to teach them. Whatever understanding that teacher has developed, whatever limitations remain in their knowledge, and whatever ability they possess to communicate and demonstrate the art will directly influence the student's development.

What happens when a teacher presents themselves as having received knowledge they never actually acquired? What happens when students invest years of their lives believing they are receiving an understanding that their instructor never took the time to develop? They may inherit movements without understanding their purpose, explanations that have never been properly examined, and ideas repeated simply because someone else said them. Eventually, some of those students may become teachers themselves, passing along the same misunderstandings to another generation. At that point, what began as an attempt to establish personal credibility has consequences far beyond the individual.

This is one of the reasons I believe we should be careful about confusing lineage with skill. A person can have a legitimate connection to a respected lineage and still possess a limited understanding of the art. Another may have developed considerable ability without having the same prestigious associations. Lineage helps us understand where knowledge came from and how it was passed along, but it does not automatically tell us how much of that knowledge someone has actually developed.

I take pride in my lineage and have tremendous respect for the teachers whose efforts made my own training possible. But their accomplishments belong to them. Their understanding does not automatically become mine because I can trace my martial arts ancestry back to them. Whatever knowledge I possess must be reflected in my own practice, and whatever I pass on to my students should come from an understanding I have genuinely worked to develop.

When I think about the experiences that have contributed most to my development, many of them are moments for which I have no photographs. The countless corrections, the hours of practice, the times I believed I understood something until my teacher demonstrated otherwise. Some of the most valuable lessons were also the most uncomfortable because they forced me to recognize limitations I did not know I had. Those experiences may not make impressive pictures to hang on a wall, but they have played a far greater role in my development than simply being photographed beside someone.

I also believe we need to be willing to turn these questions toward ourselves. Are we representing our experiences honestly? Are we giving proper credit to those who have taught us? Do we recognize the difference between something we have been shown, something we have practiced, and something we actually understand? Are we still willing to receive correction, even after years of teaching? Or have we become so concerned with maintaining a certain image that acknowledging our limitations has become difficult?

These are questions I believe every martial artist should revisit throughout their development, and I include myself in that responsibility. The longer we practice, the easier it can become to identify ourselves with our years of experience, our titles, or the people we have studied with. But none of those things should prevent us from examining the quality of our own understanding. If anything, that responsibility becomes greater when others begin looking to us for guidance.

I am not suggesting that we should stop displaying photographs of our teachers or the people who have influenced our martial arts journey. Quite the opposite. I believe preserving that history is important. Take the photographs, share the memories, and honor the relationships. But represent them honestly. If we met someone, there is nothing wrong with saying we met them. If we attended a seminar, acknowledge what we learned there. And if we were fortunate enough to spend years under someone's personal guidance, that relationship deserves to be recognized for what it was. There is dignity in each of those experiences without needing to make them appear greater than they actually were.

Ultimately, the responsibility of a teacher is not to convince students of how many important people they have met, but to help those students develop an understanding of the art. The names and faces on the wall may tell us something about a teacher's history, but they cannot establish the depth of that teacher's knowledge, the quality of their instruction, or the integrity with which they represent their experiences.

So the next time you walk into a Gwoon, take a moment to appreciate the photographs. There may be a great deal of history in those pictures, and that history deserves our respect. But remember that the person standing in front of you deserves just as much attention, if not more.

After all, reading between the pictures is not about questioning the greatness of the people hanging on the wall. It is about understanding what their presence there does, and does not, tell us about the person who put them there.

*R. González*
