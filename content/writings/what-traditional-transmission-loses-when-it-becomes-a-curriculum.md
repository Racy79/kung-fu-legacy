---
title: "What Traditional Transmission Loses When It Becomes a Curriculum"
date: "2026-08-26"
register: "tradition"
excerpt: "A curriculum can be franchised. A transmission can't."
---

I understand why so many schools eventually turn into curriculums. A curriculum scales. You can write it down, hand it to new instructors, open a second location, a third, a tenth, and know roughly what every student everywhere is receiving regardless of who happens to be teaching them that day. From a business standpoint, I don't fault anyone for building that. From a transmission standpoint, something real gets lost the moment that happens, and most people never notice it's missing because the thing that's missing doesn't show up on a syllabus.

A curriculum teaches technique. It can be extremely good at teaching technique, better in some ways than a single overworked teacher training too many students personally could ever manage. What a curriculum cannot do is transmit judgment, because judgment isn't a document. It's a thousand small decisions accumulated over years, watching one specific teacher decide when to push a student and when to let them struggle a little longer, what to say in the moment a technique still isn't landing, how to read a room of different bodies and different histories and adjust without a script. None of that survives being written down, because the moment you try to standardize it, you've already replaced the thing itself with a description of it.

This is why belt-factory schools can produce technically competent practitioners at scale and still produce almost no one who could actually teach the art the way it was taught to them. They received the curriculum. They never received the judgment underneath it, because the format they were trained in was never built to transmit that in the first place. It's not a failure of effort on anyone's part. It's a structural limitation of trying to scale something that was only ever built to travel one relationship at a time.

I'm not against curriculums. I use one myself, loosely, because students need some shape to move through. But I don't confuse the curriculum with the transmission. The curriculum is what I can write down. The transmission is everything that happens in the room that never makes it onto paper, and if I ever let the first one replace the second, I'd be running a very good technique class and calling it something it isn't.

-R Gonzalez
