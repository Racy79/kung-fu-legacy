---
title: "The Restore Function: Why Biu Gee Isn't Your Third Form"
date: "2026-08-26"
register: "technical"
excerpt: "Biu Gee isn't the next step. It's what happens when the plan already failed."
---

Most people learn Wing Chun's forms in order and assume the order tells them something about sequence. Siu Nim Tao, then Chum Kiu, then Biu Gee, one after another like chapters in a book. By that logic Biu Gee is just the advanced material, the thing you get to once you've earned it. I used to think that too, until I noticed how differently Biu Gee actually behaves compared to the two forms before it.

Siu Nim Tao establishes structure. Chum Kiu maintains that structure while things start moving. Both of those forms assume the plan is working. You're standing where you should be standing, your structure is intact, and you're operating inside the system the way it's meant to operate. Biu Gee assumes none of that. Biu Gee assumes you're already compromised, out of position, structure broken, and it exists to answer one question: what do you do when the primary system has already failed.

That's not a third step in a sequence. That's a parallel system that only activates on failure. You don't move through Biu Gee the way you move through the first two forms. You reach for it the way you'd reach for anything built for an emergency, which is to say you hope you rarely need it, but you train it obsessively so that when you do need it, it's already there without having to think your way to it.

I think this gets missed because forms get taught as a curriculum instead of as a system. A curriculum has a next step. A system has functions, and functions don't all activate at the same time or in the same order. Once you see Biu Gee as the restore function instead of the third form, a lot of techniques inside it stop looking strange. The elbows, the recovery angles, the way it seems to break its own rules compared to the first two forms, none of that is Biu Gee being advanced. It's Biu Gee being honest about what it's actually for.

I don't teach Biu Gee as a reward for finishing Chum Kiu. I teach it as the thing you reach for when everything else has already gone wrong, because that's what it is, and pretending otherwise just delays the day you actually need it and don't have it.

-R Gonzalez
