---
title: "On Being Corrected: The Skill Most Students Never Learn"
date: "2026-09-04"
register: "tradition"
excerpt: "How someone receives a correction tells you more than the correction itself."
---

Nobody arrives already good at being corrected. It has to be learned, usually slowly, usually with some resistance along the way, and I'd argue it's the single most important skill a student develops, more important in the long run than any technique I could teach them, because a student who's actually good at being corrected will eventually fix everything else on their own.

Most people's first instinct when corrected is some version of defense. Explain why they did it that way, point out that it worked before, mention the fatigue or the distraction that caused the mistake. None of that is malicious. It's just the ordinary human reflex to protect the self from the discomfort of being wrong in front of someone, and I don't hold it against anyone the first hundred times it happens. But there's a difference between a reflex and a pattern, and the students who never move past the reflex are the ones who stop developing, no matter how much raw talent they started with.

Being genuinely correctable requires something that looks passive from the outside but isn't. It requires separating the correction from your worth, hearing "this is wrong" without hearing "you are inadequate," and that separation is much harder than it sounds, because most of us were raised in contexts where the two got fused together early and often. Learning to unfuse them, to hear a correction as information about a movement instead of a verdict on a person, is genuinely a skill, and like every skill, it improves with deliberate practice, not by accident.

I watch for this constantly, more than I watch for technical aptitude, because technical aptitude without correctability has a ceiling, and that ceiling arrives faster than people expect. The most gifted student I've ever taught wasn't the most naturally talented. He was the one who could hear the same correction fifteen times, feel embarrassed exactly zero of those times, and just adjust, again, without needing the moment to mean anything more than what it actually was.

If you want to know how far someone will go in this art, or honestly in any serious craft, don't watch what they do when they're right. Watch what happens on their face in the half-second after they're told they're wrong.

-R Gonzalez
