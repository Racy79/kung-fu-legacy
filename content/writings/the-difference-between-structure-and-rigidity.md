---
title: "The Difference Between Structure and Rigidity"
date: "2026-08-30"
register: "technical"
excerpt: "One survives contact. The other breaks the moment it meets resistance."
---

The most common critique I hear about internal arts, usually from people who train something more overtly athletic, is that it looks stiff, too formal, too composed to be real fighting. I understand where that comes from. From the outside, structure and rigidity look almost identical. They are not close to the same thing, and confusing the two is the fastest way to build something that fails the first time it actually gets tested.

Rigidity is a shape you hold. Structure is a relationship you maintain. That distinction sounds abstract until you feel the difference under pressure, and then it becomes the only thing that matters. A rigid position resists force by refusing to move at all, which works exactly until the force exceeds what the position can absorb, at which point it breaks all at once, usually somewhere you didn't expect. A structured position absorbs and redirects force continuously, adjusting in real time while never losing its center. It bends without collapsing, because bending isn't the failure. Losing the center is the failure.

I test this with students constantly, usually without them realizing it's a test. I'll apply pressure somewhere unexpected during a drill and watch what happens. Rigid students brace harder, which usually makes things worse, because they're fighting the pressure directly instead of redirecting it. Structured students absorb it, find where their base is compromised, and correct without ever losing contact or center. The rigid response looks strong for about half a second. The structured response looks unremarkable and works every time.

This is also why structure takes so much longer to develop than rigidity does. Rigidity you can fake almost immediately, just tense everything and hold still. Structure requires your body to understand, at a level below conscious thought, exactly how much tension is necessary and where, and not one ounce more than that. That's not a strength question. It's a sensitivity question, and sensitivity is slow to build because it can't be forced, only refined through repetition until it becomes automatic.

When people say internal arts don't look real, what they're often looking at is early-stage structure that hasn't matured yet, or worse, rigidity being mistaken for structure by an instructor who never made the distinction themselves. Real structure doesn't look impressive from the outside. It looks like almost nothing happened, right up until you're the one applying the pressure and feeling it disappear.

-R Gonzalez
